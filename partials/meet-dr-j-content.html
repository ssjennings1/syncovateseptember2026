Let's Talk
Dr. Shannon Jennings — portrait

The problem people bring you is almost never the real problem.

I learned that early, working with families in the hardest situations — divorce, custody, the stuff where somebody's kids are on the line. The real problem was the structure around them. The unspoken rules. What everybody knew behind closed doors but nobody said in the hallway.

I carried that into business at the Edward Lowe Foundation, running peer retreats for entrepreneurs and CEOs. Their whole mission was to champion the entrepreneurial spirit — and I'm still living that mission more than a decade after I left. Because these companies matter. The ones growing in your town, hiring locally, sponsoring the little leagues, putting money back in — they hold communities together, especially now, when a lot of other things are falling apart. That's who I've built my work around.

Here's what I've learned in all those rooms: when you build something, you're in it. The business and the person aren't two separate things. Your money, your identity, your relationships, how you sleep — it's all tied up in the thing you made. That's true for the trades contractor and the manufacturer and the professional-services owner, and it gets even more tangled in a family business, where the succession talk is also a family talk and the org chart and the dinner table are the same table. You have to see the whole picture to be any real use in any of it. That's the work I love most.

Why a Business Psychologist?

My doctorate in business psychology put real, evidence-based structure around what twenty years of closed doors had already taught me.

I kept seeing the same thing: leaders with the best of intentions, working incredibly hard, and their teams were fracturing and the work was stalling out. The assumption was always a people problem — fix the difficult personality, hire better talent.

Look closer and it's rarely a people problem. It's an architecture problem.

When you build a company from scratch, the system organizes itself around you. The processes, the communication patterns, the way authority flows — all of it made sense when the company was small. Then the business grows, and the structure that helped it survive starts to suffocate it. You built it, which makes you the worst person to see what's broken in it. Not because anything's wrong with you. Because you're inside it.

You can't fix what you can't see from inside. My job is to be the person who can see the architecture of your blind spots — and stay in the room until something actually moves.

Direct. Not one-sided. In it with you.

If you want someone to nod along and tell you the plan looks great, I'm not the right fit.

  • I listen first.

    Long enough that you'll probably say the thing you weren't planning to say. Then I say my piece, plainly.

  • I don't take sides.

    Not the owner's, not the team's, not the family's. That's usually why people open up in the first place.

  • I won't talk down to you.

    If you built your leadership by doing it instead of studying it, you know more than you're giving yourself credit for. I'm not here to replace what you've got. I'm here to help you see it clearly and use it better.

  • I stay.

    Insight is the easy part. The hard part is next Tuesday, when you have to do something with it. I'm in the room for that, too.

The work takes whatever shape the situation calls for. One session to get a stuck decision unstuck. A year of monthly calls that become the one place you can think out loud without consequences. Me in a room with your team when they've stopped being honest with each other. What it isn't: therapy — that's a different practice — or a program you work through on a schedule. And when I stop being useful, I'll say so.
